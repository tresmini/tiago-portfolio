### S001 — Cloud Validator for False Positive Reduction
**Company:** Cobli | **Role:** PM, AI Squad
**Primary Skill:** Technical depth | **Secondary Skill:** Strategic thinking
**Impact:** ~70% reduction in false positive driving events surfaced to customers

Cobli Cam's dashcam embedded computer vision algorithm generated a high rate of false positives for risky driving events — unsafe distance, drowsiness, distraction. Customers couldn't trust the detections and were manually reviewing every flagged video. Internal labeling revealed some models had precision as low as 30%; some customers were marking 90%+ of alerts as irrelevant. The firmware was owned by the dashcam supplier — we had no ability to modify the underlying algorithm.

As PM of the AI squad, my task was to reduce false positive rates to a level where customers could trust and act on alerts without reviewing everything.

I started by labeling videos to establish baseline precision and build training datasets. I built a prioritization framework combining three factors: baseline precision, engineering effort, and customer-reported importance (derived from interviews and event review rates). Based on this, I prioritized the unsafe distance model first — lowest precision, highest customer complaint rate, and technically feasible via depth-inferring models.

The squad ran experiments with multiple approaches, iterated through versions, and reached 95% precision and 75% recall on the cloud validator. The model sat on top of the embedded algorithm as a filter: events flagged by the camera were validated by the cloud model before surfacing to customers.

The cloud validator achieved 95% precision and 75% recall — catching approximately 70% of false positives generated by the embedded firmware.

**Key Insight:** When you can't fix an AI system you don't control, building a validation layer on top isn't just a patch — it's a forcing function for creating labeled data at scale. That labeled dataset is what's actually hard to copy: it compounds over time, trains future models, and gives you the path to eventually owning what you were dependent on.

### S002 — Ad Platform Migration
**Company:** OLX | **Role:** PM, Ad Management + Ad Creation Squads
**Primary Skill:** Execution/delivery | **Secondary Skill:** Influence without authority
**Impact:** -80% reduction in development steps for the most complex use case; from ~20 steps to 4

OLX's marketplace verticals — autos, real estate, goods — each had multiple squads building end-user experiences, all blocked by the same bottleneck: the core "ad" entity was fragmented across ~5 legacy microservices, each owned by a different squad. Adding a single new product category required coordinating changes across all 5 services — each step taking 1-3 days, with unfamiliar codebases and multiple review queues. Vertical roadmaps were effectively held hostage by infrastructure they didn't own.

As PM of the Ad Management and Ad Creation squads, my task was to build a modern ad platform with clean external APIs that abstracted the legacy complexity — letting vertical teams ship new use cases independently.

The hardest part wasn't technical. Platform investments are long-haul: they take months to build, generate no visible user-facing output for most of that time, and require sustained management conviction to survive quarterly prioritization pressure.

My first move was to invent a measurement system: I mapped existing use case workflows step-by-step and defined "workflow steps" as the proxy metric — each step being 1-3 days of real, non-trivial engineering work. I used this to build a concrete, ongoing business case for management, showing measurable progress at each iteration rather than asking for trust until full delivery.

I also ran discovery with all vertical PMs to understand future roadmap needs, ensuring the architecture would support what teams needed to build next. And I enforced adoption through policy: no legacy updates accepted once the new platform could support the use case.

The result: -80% reduction in development steps for the most complex use case. A change that previously required coordinating ~5 services could be executed via a single API call.

**Key Insight:** Platform teams get consumed by technical complexity — and the complexity is real. But the biggest PM failure mode in platform investments isn't technical; it's losing stakeholder buy-in mid-build because the team has nothing to show. The PM's job is to manufacture visible, measurable progress at each iteration — not just deliver the completed platform at the end.

### S003 — Analytics Query Latency
**Company:** RD Station | **Role:** PM, Analytics & Reports Squad
**Primary Skill:** Problem-solving | **Secondary Skill:** Data-driven decision making
**Impact:** p90 query latency reduced from 30s+ to ~3s (~90% reduction); data integrity 100%

Shortly after joining RD Station as PM of the Analytics & Reports squad, customer feedback surfaced that reports weren't loading. Investigation revealed p90 latency on a key query exceeded 30 seconds — past the browser's timeout threshold, causing reports to crash entirely. The affected customers were disproportionately the largest accounts: more data meant higher latency, meaning the customers most at risk of churning were experiencing the worst performance. Root cause: analytical queries running directly on a transactional PostgreSQL database not designed for high-scale analytical workloads.

My first move was to establish that the problem was solvable — not to define how. I used industry benchmarks: competitors with much larger datasets weren't experiencing 30-second report latency. That meant the constraint was our implementation, not our ambition. I gave engineers a dedicated research sprint. They proposed a streaming service for events feeding a data warehouse with query-optimized table modelling.

Rather than committing to a 3-month build immediately, I proposed a prototype first: 1-2 tables, 3-4 most-used queries, 1 week of data. The prototype would either confirm the approach or surface problems before the full investment. It confirmed the direction.

Delivered in 3 months with data integrity validated at 100%. p90 dropped from 30s+ to ~3s.

**Key Insight:** PMs don't need to know the technical solution — but they need to know two things: that the problem is solvable (use benchmarks; if competitors solved it, you can too), and how to break the path to solution into units that generate learning before the full commitment lands.

### S004 — UX Retention Correlation Analysis
**Company:** Cobli | **Role:** PM, AI Squad (self-initiated cross-squad project)
**Primary Skill:** Influence without authority | **Secondary Skill:** Data-driven decision making
**Impact:** UX teams gained roadmap space; "breadth of usage" adopted as company-wide metric; +2pp MRR retention in following period

UX-focused PMs and designers at Cobli were stuck in a recurring pattern: they had strong qualitative evidence that UX quality was poor and likely affecting adoption and retention. But in quarterly planning, management consistently prioritized new feature requests from sales and CS over UX iteration work. The qualitative case wasn't landing.

This wasn't my squad. Nobody asked me to fix it. But as a senior PM hired partly to raise the product management bar, I saw it as a systemic problem worth solving.

I ran a 12-month cohort analysis: pulled 8 custom Mixpanel events representing meaningful usage across the product's main features, synthesized them into 5 value propositions grouping related user journeys, and gathered MRR plus upsell/downsell data for each customer over the same period. I calculated net revenue retention across different customer cohorts and tested multiple hypotheses about which combinations of feature usage correlated with positive NRR (above 100%).

The analysis found meaningful correlations between specific usage combinations and retention outcomes. I proposed a new high-level product metric, "breadth of usage," capturing how broadly customers engaged across the platform's value propositions.

UX teams gained roadmap space. "Breadth of usage" was adopted as a strategic planning metric company-wide. In the following period, MRR retention improved by 2 percentage points.

**Key Insight:** UX teams lose roadmap battles not because management doesn't value UX — they lose because they present the problem in the language of someone already convinced. The fix isn't better advocacy — it's translating intuition into the data language of someone who needs to see the retention correlation before they'll believe it.

### S005 — First-Party Web Tracker
**Company:** RD Station | **Role:** PM, Analytics & Reports Squad
**Primary Skill:** Strategic thinking | **Secondary Skill:** Execution/delivery
**Impact:** 99% precision in controlled conditions; dedicated squad hired; 3-month silent pre-collection rollout established

RD Station's top-of-funnel metrics had been sourced from a Google Analytics integration since the company's founding. Three failure modes were eroding customer trust: customers integrating the wrong GA project by accident; forgetting to install the GA tracker on landing pages; and GA's unpredictable data latency conflicting with RD Station's near-real-time data — producing impossible-looking results.

The obvious move was to fix the GA integration. I chose a different argument: replace it entirely.

My pitch rested on four pillars: customer satisfaction evidence (support tickets, NPS comments, screenshots of contradictory data); the strategic argument (GA gave aggregated pageview counts, but owning raw events was the infrastructure prerequisite for RD Station's Customer Data Platform vision — the difference between "20 visitors today" and "this specific lead visited your pricing page 3 minutes ago"); the forcing function (Google Analytics was deprecating their current version, so engineering work was required regardless); and the precedent cost (every quarter without owning raw event data was a quarter of CDP capability permanently deferred).

The pitch secured approval. We hired a dedicated squad (3 developers + team leader). We reached 99% precision compared to GA in controlled conditions.

**Key Insight:** Most teams evaluate third-party integrations for what they provide today, not for what they permanently foreclose. The right question isn't "does this meet our current needs?" — it's "what use cases become impossible if we never own this data?"

### S006 — ZAP+ Integration Crisis
**Company:** OLX | **Role:** PM, Ad Management + Ad Creation Squads
**Primary Skill:** Ambiguity navigation | **Secondary Skill:** Problem-solving
**Impact:** Accounts with integrity problems reduced from 15% to 1%

Following OLX's R$3 billion acquisition of ZAP, the flagship product was ZAP+ — a subscription publishing ads across three marketplaces simultaneously. When first customers signed up, support tickets spiked: ads published in ZAP weren't appearing in OLX. Both VPs of Engineering and Heads of Engineering from both business units were in a warroom. Despite the seniority in the room, nobody had a clear view of what was actually broken.

I was called in as PM of OLX's ads core squad to help diagnose and resolve the integration problem.

The room was operating on unstructured, qualitative data — specific examples, but no way to measure scale or track progress. My first action: personally built a SQL query joining both OLX and ZAP data warehouses, comparing the status of mirrored ads across both platforms. This created two things: a quantitative integrity metric (% of accounts with at least one ad with an integrity problem — baseline: 15%) and a selection mechanism for specific cases to investigate.

Using the query to select representative cases, I structured debugging warrooms with both squads. The actual root cause emerged: OLX and ZAP had different ad lifecycle models. ZAP had an "unpublish without delete" feature that OLX didn't. When customers used it, ZAP was calling OLX's delete API — which either permanently deleted the ad or was blocked by OLX business rules.

Accounts with integrity problems dropped from 15% to 1%.

**Key Insight:** In high-pressure warrooms, the default is to start debugging based on the most senior person's best guess. Without a measurement framework, you can't tell if you're making progress or even if you're pulling the right thread. The discipline nobody wants to exercise under pressure is pausing to define what "fixed" looks like quantitatively before touching anything.

### S007 — Facial Recognition Precision Improvement
**Company:** Cobli | **Role:** PM, AI Squad
**Primary Skill:** Technical depth | **Secondary Skill:** Problem-solving
**Impact:** Precision improved from ~80% to 98% in production; churning customer stabilized; deployed to all customers June 2025

Cobli's fleet management platform included a facial recognition feature for driver identification — critical for accountability on behavior events like speeding or harsh braking. When I joined the squad as PM, complaints about wrong driver assignments existed, but I initially deprioritized them against other roadmap pressures. In late 2024, one important customer threatened to churn specifically due to driver misidentification — including gender mismatches. A manual data labeling exercise revealed overall precision around 80%, well below acceptable thresholds.

I broke the problem into three hypotheses: model quality, image quality, and partial obstructions. We isolated model quality first — selecting a clean dataset and benchmarking top-of-market facial recognition models against ours. We found a critical blind spot: our model degraded sharply on colorless images produced by a recently launched dashcam with infrared lenses. A new dashcam hardware launch had silently introduced a new image type the model was never trained on.

Testing on a realistic dataset revealed precision problems persisted — so we ran two parallel quality-filtering experiments: one using open-source image processing algorithms scoring resolution, sharpness, brightness, and contrast; another using the face-detection model's built-in confidence score as a quality proxy. Combined with the new model, filtering low-quality images pushed precision to 98%.

We made a deliberate product call: prioritize precision over recall. Wrong identifications destroy trust completely and irreversibly. Non-identifications are understandable and recoverable.

Deployed to all customers by June 2025. The churning customer stabilized. Escalations dropped across complaining accounts.

**Key Insight:** In AI-powered features, the type of error matters more than aggregate accuracy. A wrong identification destroys trust completely and irreversibly. A non-identification is understandable and forgettable. When forced to trade precision for recall, you're not making a technical call — you're making a trust architecture decision.

### S008 — Analytics Satisfaction Improvement
**Company:** RD Station | **Role:** PM, Analytics & Reports Squad
**Primary Skill:** Product strategy | **Secondary Skill:** User research
**Impact:** Satisfaction 2.8 → 4.2 (+50%); engagement (events per session) +30%; MAU 20% → 25%; 3-month timeline

After nearly two years as PM of Analytics & Reports at RD Station, I had resolved the most pressing technical and reliability issues. But the engagement picture remained troubling: only 20% of accounts were monthly active on analytics features.

The engaged cohort split into two groups: analytics-dependent customers who used what existed but complained loudly that it was too shallow (constantly exporting raw data to Excel); and data-immature customers who simply didn't engage. Analytics had one of the worst NPS tag scores across the product, and customer success had flagged analytics complaints as one of the top churn risk drivers among highest-paying accounts.

I faced a classic prioritization question: invest in the 80% non-engaged, or the 20% unhappy-but-active?

My thesis: the engaged 20% showed surface retention but not satisfaction. They used the feature because they had no alternative — they were stuck, not satisfied. Investing in growing the 80% would just expose more customers to a product without product-market fit. The right path was to establish PMF with the engaged cohort first.

I designed a two-question in-product survey separating satisfaction with the result of landing page analysis from satisfaction with the time spent doing it. Baseline: 2.8 out of 5 on the landing page report. I recruited 5 heavy-user marketing agency accounts as standing design partners and ran a weekly delivery cadence: ship a subfeature → roll out to focus group → collect feedback → iterate.

After 3 months: satisfaction 2.8 → 4.2 (+50%). Engagement (events per session) +30%. Monthly active accounts: 20% → 25% (+5pp).

**Key Insight:** Surface engagement from your most active users isn't proof of PMF — it's often the opposite signal. Power users who return regularly but complain, export all their data, and build workarounds aren't loyal customers; they're stuck ones.
